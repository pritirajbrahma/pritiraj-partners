The Half That Does Not Show Up — India's Female Participation Problem

Pritiraj BrahmaFounding Partner, Pritiraj & Partners · 5 March 2026

Female labour force participation has risen to 40%. Urban female participation sits near 25%, and 44.4% of women outside the workforce name care work as the reason. The constraint is not ambition.

India's female participation number has improved substantially and is still describing a workforce most women do not enter. The reason they give is not preference. It is care.

The Periodic Labour Force Survey Annual Report for 2025 puts the labour force participation rate for persons aged 15 and above at 59.3 per cent — 79.1 per cent for men and 40.0 per cent for women. The worker population ratio stands at 57.4 per cent overall, 76.6 per cent for men and 38.8 per cent for women.

Forty per cent is a genuine improvement on the sub-25 per cent figures of the late 2010s, and the improvement should be credited. It also leaves India with one of the widest participation gaps among large economies, and the composition of the improvement matters as much as its size.

Four findings anchor this analysis:

  1. The stated reason is care, and it is stated overwhelmingly. Among men outside the labour force, 69.8 per cent cite continuing education. Among women, 44.4 per cent cite child care and personal commitments in home-making. Two populations, two entirely different constraints, one undifferentiated policy response.
  2. The gain is rural, and the urban number has barely moved. In December 2025, female LFPR stood at 40.1 per cent in rural areas against 25.3 per cent in urban areas. Rural female WPR was 38.6 per cent against urban female WPR of about 23.0 per cent. India's cities — where formal jobs, transport and childcare markets exist — perform worse than its villages.
  3. Educated young urban women face the highest unemployment of any group. Urban young women record an unemployment rate of 18.9 per cent against 11.8 per cent for urban young men. These are women who have entered the labour force and cannot find work — a different failure from non-participation, and one that discourages the next cohort from entering at all.
  4. Much rural participation is self-employment and unpaid family labour. Across the workforce, the self-employed share is around 56 per cent and regular wage or salaried work about 24 per cent. Rural female participation rises substantially through work on family holdings, which counts as participation and does not necessarily deliver independent income.

Why the urban number is the diagnostic one

If the barrier to women's work were cultural preference alone, urban participation should exceed rural — cities are less traditional, better educated and richer. India shows the opposite. That inverted pattern is the strongest available evidence that the binding constraint is practical rather than attitudinal.

Three practical constraints dominate, and each has a named owner in government.

Care. Formal childcare and elder care are scarce, expensive, and mostly unregulated. In a rural joint household, care is absorbed informally; in a nuclear urban household it must be purchased, and at prevailing entry-level wages the purchase is not worth making. A woman's decision not to work is often an arithmetic conclusion, not a cultural one.

Safety and transport. Commute risk falls disproportionately on women and functions as a tax on the wage. The effect is well documented in the evaluations of free bus travel schemes in several states, where ridership and reported work-seeking both rose.

Job structure. Formal employment in India is built around continuous full-time attendance with no accommodation for a career interrupted by care. A woman who exits at childbirth finds re-entry priced as though her prior experience did not exist.

The honest counter-case

A serious objection: some of the measured increase reflects better measurement rather than more work. The revamped PLFS design, with monthly bulletins and a calendar-year cycle from 2025, captures activity that earlier rounds classified as domestic duty. If so, part of the improvement is statistical.

That cuts both ways, and honestly stated it strengthens rather than weakens the case: if women were always working and India was failing to count it, then the policy failure is older and larger than the participation series suggests.

What we would do

  1. Treat childcare as economic infrastructure, and fund it as such. Not as a welfare scheme for children but as a labour-supply intervention for mothers, evaluated on female employment outcomes. Anganwadi infrastructure exists in almost every village and could carry a full-day component with staffing and hours designed around a working day.
  2. Set an explicit urban target with a named owner. Urban female participation near 25 per cent is the specific failure. It should be a published state target with a department accountable for it, not a line in an aggregate.
  3. Make transport safety a measured output. Lit stops, last-mile connectivity, staffed interchanges — reported per corridor, with women's ridership as the outcome measure.
  4. Legislate re-entry. Returnship provisions in public employment and public procurement conditions, so that a career gap for care is not treated as a competence gap.
  5. Separate self-employment from unpaid family labour in reporting. Participation that generates no independent income is a different economic fact from participation that does. Reporting them together flatters the number and obscures the policy question.

India's demographic dividend arithmetic does not work with 40 per cent of its women in the labour force and a quarter of them in its cities. No country has converted a demographic window while leaving half its working-age population outside the economy — and India's own data now names the reason, in the words of the women themselves.
