Every Tap Was Counted. Not Every Tap Ran.

A sample edition of the Last Mile Index, run on the largest rural drinking-water programme in the world. The instrument does one thing: it follows a scheme through the stages between sanction and citizen, and reports the stage at which the published number stops meaning what it appears to mean.

Prepared 3 September 2026 · Figures as published to that date; the connection series is a live dashboard and moves daily · Every figure below is attributed. Where two official sources disagree, both are given.

India connected 12.58 crore rural households to a tap in under seven years — an infrastructure expansion with no peer in the world. The Government's own 2024 functionality assessment then found that in certified villages, 98 per cent of households had a tap and only about three in four received water meeting the Mission's own standards of quantity, quality and regularity. Both facts are true. Only one of them is on the dashboard.

What the instrument measures

Every delivery programme passes through the same seven gates. A scheme can be exemplary at gate three and fail at gate five, and the published headline will still be gate three. The Index scores each gate separately, states which document the score comes from, and names the gate where reporting and reality separate.

The finding

The programme built the asset and, for five years, certified the asset as the service.

Stage 03 is a genuine achievement and the Index says so: 12.58 crore connections in under seven years is the fastest rural infrastructure expansion India has executed. The failure is narrower and more correctable than critics allow. A connection was recorded when the tap was installed; the Mission's own service standard — 55 litres per capita per day, of prescribed quality, regularly — was measured only by periodic third-party survey, and never by the reporting line that produced the headline. So the dashboard rose to 81.71% while the share of households actually receiving water to standard sat near three in four, and both numbers were the Government's own.

The honest counter-case

Two objections deserve to be stated. First, functionality is inherently harder to report than installation: a connection is a one-time event, service is a daily condition, and no country reports daily rural service delivery household by household. Second, the state has moved — JJM 2.0 makes operations and maintenance a condition of certification rather than a subsequent aspiration, which is the correct structural fix, and Jal Seva Aankalan shifts assessment from an occasional external survey to a standing local one. The Index's position is not that the gap was concealed. It is that the gap was published in one document and the headline in another, and only one of the two was ever quoted.

What we would do

  1. Report the service number beside the connection number, on the same dashboard, from the same source. One line, two figures: households connected, and households receiving water to the 55 lpcd standard in the last seven days. If the second cannot be produced monthly, publish it quarterly and say when it was last refreshed.
  2. Make certification revocable. A Har Ghar Jal village that fails a functionality assessment should lose the status until it passes again. A certification that can only ever be earned, never lost, is a completion record, not a service measure.
  3. Publish denominators with every percentage. The launch baseline exists in two versions because the household count changed and no release reconciled them. A percentage without its denominator is not a statistic.
  4. Give every certified village a named O&M owner and a published user-charge decision. JJM 2.0 requires the arrangement; publishing who holds it is what makes it enforceable by the household rather than by the auditor.
  5. Fund the assessment, not only the asset. Set the functionality assessment as a standing budget line with a fixed publication calendar, so it survives the year in which the capital works finish.
