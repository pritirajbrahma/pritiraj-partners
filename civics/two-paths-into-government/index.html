Two paths into government: the examination and the ballot

A citizen who wants to govern has two doors. One is opened by an examination and closes only at retirement; the other is opened by an electorate and closes every five years. The difference explains most of how Indian government behaves.

One door is opened by an examination and closes at retirement. The other is opened by an electorate and closes every five years.

01

The examination

Entry is by competitive examination conducted by a constitutional commission, and tenure runs to retirement. The rules of dismissal are themselves constitutional, not administrative.

02

The ballot

Entry is by election in a constituency, and tenure runs to the end of the term or the loss of the House. Accountability is continuous and public, and it is renewed or withdrawn by voters.

03

The same building

Both paths end in the same room, holding different instruments: one supplies continuity and the record, the other supplies the mandate and the decision. The friction between them is the design, not a fault.

File 04 — connecting the dots

Two paths a citizen can take

Everyone in the earlier files started here: an ordinary citizen. One path is won by examination and lasts a career. The other is won by votes and lasts until the next election. They end up in the same room.
Path one — appointed
The examination
IAS, IPS, IFS and the rest of the permanent executive
Path two — elected
The ballot
MLA, MP, minister, Chief Minister, Prime Minister
A1
Who may sit the exam
Any graduate, generally between 21 and 32, with a limited number of attempts. Upper age and attempts are relaxed for reserved categories and for candidates with disabilities. The IAS and IPS are open only to Indian citizens.
No political affiliation, no party membership, no constituency. Merit and preference decide everything from here.
A2
Preliminary examination
Two objective papers — general studies and an aptitude test — held by the Union Public Service Commission. It is a screening round only: the marks do not carry forward. States run their own equivalent through a State Public Service Commission.
A3
Main examination
Nine written papers over several days: an essay, four general studies papers, two papers in a chosen optional subject, and two qualifying language papers. Everything is written by hand and marked descriptively.
A4
Personality test
An interview before a UPSC board. The written papers and the interview together produce one national merit list, published rank by rank.
A5
Service and cadre
Rank and stated preference decide the service — IAS, IPS, Indian Foreign Service, revenue and audit services — and the cadre state a candidate will spend a career in. Neither is a matter of choice alone.
A6
Training
A common foundation course, then service academy training — the IAS at Mussoorie, the IPS at Hyderabad — followed by district attachment under a serving officer.
A7
The career ladder
Sub-Divisional Magistrate — first posting
District Collector or Superintendent of Police
Department Secretary in the state
Joint and Additional Secretary, Government of India
Secretary to a ministry
Cabinet Secretary — the top of the service
Promotion by seniority and empanelment, retirement at sixty. No election ever touches this ladder.
B1
Who may stand
Any citizen on the electoral roll, aged 25 or more for the Lok Sabha or a Legislative Assembly. No degree, no examination and no upper age limit.
Disqualified: an office of profit under the government, undischarged insolvency, and conviction with a sentence of two years or more.
B2
Building a base
Most careers start locally: booth and ward work for a party, a youth or student wing, a panchayat or municipal seat, a union or a movement. Some candidates arrive instead with standing earned entirely outside politics.
B3
The ticket
A party nominates one candidate per constituency and lends its symbol. This is the real bottleneck of the elected path — an internal party decision with no published criteria. Anyone refused can still stand as an independent.
B4
Nomination and campaign
Papers filed with the Returning Officer, a security deposit, and an affidavit of assets and criminal cases. Then a campaign under the Model Code of Conduct, with spending capped and accounted for.
B5
The poll
Secret ballot, first past the post. Whoever leads the count wins the seat, majority or not — and one constituency's verdict is the entire qualification for office.
B6
Sworn in as a member
The oath is taken before the presiding officer of the House. A five-year term, a constituency to answer to, and a party whip to follow — defying it can cost the seat under the anti-defection law.
B7
The career ladder
Member of the House — MP or MLA
Committee member, party whip
Deputy Minister or Minister of State
Cabinet Minister with a portfolio
Leader of the legislature party
Chief Minister or Prime Minister
No seniority rule and no retirement age. Every rung depends on winning again, and on the leader's confidence.
C

Where the two paths meet

Each pair works in the same office, one elected, one appointed
In a district
MLA  ·  Collector
The MLA carries the constituency's demands and the political mandate. The Collector runs revenue, land, relief and coordination, and stays when the MLA changes.
In a ministry
Minister  ·  Secretary
The minister decides and answers in the House. The secretary is the ministry's official head: advises frankly, records the advice, then carries out whatever is decided.
At the top
PM  ·  Cabinet Secretary
The Prime Minister chairs the Cabinet; the Cabinet Secretary services it, coordinates every ministry, and hands the same machinery to the next government.
The difference
Appointed — the service
Elected — the politician
Selected by
A competitive examination and a national merit list
A party ticket and then the voters of one constituency
Tenure
Until sixty, with constitutional protection against arbitrary removal
Five years at a time, and only while the House keeps confidence
Answers to
The minister of the day, service conduct rules and the courts
The House, the party whip and the electorate
Decides
How a policy is implemented, and advises on what is feasible
What the policy will be, and takes the responsibility for it
The dot that connects everything
One citizen, two doors
The elected side supplies the mandate and can be voted out. The appointed side supplies continuity and cannot be. Every structure in File 01, every step in File 02 and every stage in File 03 is filled by people who came through one of these two doors.
