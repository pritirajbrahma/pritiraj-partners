The Largest Thing India Does Well

Pritiraj BrahmaFounding Partner, Pritiraj & Partners · 1 February 2026

India moves 64 crore people through a voting booth in six weeks without serious incident — the most impressive administrative act on earth. Everything surrounding that act is weaker than the act itself.

ProvenancePublished 1 February 2026. Last substantively revised 10 August 2026.

India performs the single most difficult logistical operation any democracy has ever attempted, and performs it superbly. The institutions that decide what the vote is worth have not kept pace with the machinery that collects it.

Begin with the thing we do not say often enough, because a journal that intends to be critical should first be accurate about what works.

In the summer of 2024, the Election Commission of India moved roughly 64 crore people through a voting booth across seven phases between 19 April and 1 June — a turnout of about 65.8 per cent from an electoral roll of some 96.8 crore. It did this across more than ten lakh polling stations, in a country where the operating rule is that no voter should have to travel more than two kilometres to vote, which means polling parties walk into forests, cross rivers, climb to villages at altitude, and in at least one celebrated case each cycle set up a booth for a single elector. Presiding officers carry machines into places the state otherwise reaches only occasionally.

There is no comparable administrative act anywhere on earth. Not in scale, not in compression, not in the near-total absence of the breakdown that scale of this kind ought to produce. It is the largest thing India does, and it is among the few things India does at global standard.

This essay is about everything that surrounds that act, because that is where the weakness is — and because a machine this good deserves institutions to match it.

Four findings anchor this analysis:

  1. The act of voting is solved; the register is not. The single greatest determinant of whether a citizen votes is not the polling station — India has made that nearly frictionless — but whether their name is on the roll, correctly, at the address where they now live. The roll is maintained continuously, revised periodically, and is the part of the system most vulnerable to both error and dispute.
  2. Representation has been frozen for half a century. The number of Lok Sabha seats has stood at 543 since the delimitation that followed the 1971 census. Every subsequent census has been excluded from seat allocation by successive constitutional freezes. India has therefore been electing a house whose composition reflects a population distribution more than fifty years old, while the population itself has roughly tripled and redistributed itself dramatically across states.
  3. The financing of politics has been ruled on but not rebuilt. In February 2024 the Supreme Court struck down the electoral bonds scheme, holding that anonymous corporate funding of political parties violated the voter's right to information under Article 19(1)(a). That judgment removed an instrument. It did not create the transparent financing architecture that should replace it, and that architecture still does not exist.
  4. Reform energy is concentrated on the calendar, not the substance. The most advanced electoral reform proposal in the country is the simultaneous elections agenda — the high-level committee's report and the constitutional amendment that followed, now under parliamentary examination. It is an argument about when elections are held. The harder questions concern what happens between them.

Why the roll is the real battleground

Every democracy's integrity rests on a list. India's list contains close to a billion names, has to be maintained in a country where tens of millions of people move for work, and must be simultaneously inclusive enough that no citizen is excluded and accurate enough that no name appears twice.

Those two requirements pull against each other, and the tension is not technical but political. Any serious cleaning of a roll removes names, and a removed name is indistinguishable — from outside — between a duplicate correctly deleted and a citizen wrongly struck off. Any serious expansion adds names, and an added name is indistinguishable between an enfranchised citizen and an improper entry. This is why roll revision, in every state and under every government, produces immediate accusations from whichever side expects to lose.

The resolution is not a better algorithm. It is procedural: publish the additions and deletions in full, with reasons, at a granularity that allows independent verification; give a struck-off citizen a fast, real appeal rather than a form; and conduct revisions on a fixed statutory calendar rather than in the run-up to a contest. A roll revised in public on a known schedule is defensible. One revised quietly close to an election will be contested whether or not it was done correctly — and the contest itself corrodes the thing the Commission has spent seventy years building.

The interval problem

India's democratic energy is overwhelmingly concentrated in the campaign. Enormous organisational and financial resources are mobilised for six weeks, and then the machinery — of parties, of media attention, of citizen participation — largely stands down until the next contest.

This is the deepest structural weakness in Indian democracy, and it is barely discussed. A member of a legislature is accountable at an election and only weakly accountable between elections: constituency development work is discretionary, attendance and legislative participation are erratically reported, and there is no routine mechanism by which a citizen learns what their representative did with five years. The Tenth Schedule binds members to their party on the floor; almost nothing binds them to their constituency in the interval.

The simultaneous elections debate is, in this light, an argument about reducing the frequency of the only moment of accountability that currently functions. There are respectable arguments for it — cost, administrative disruption, the perpetual campaign's effect on governance. But the case would be much stronger if it arrived alongside a mechanism that made the interval accountable. Fewer elections plus unchanged interval accountability is a net reduction in the citizen's leverage, whatever its administrative merits.

Money, after the bonds

The electoral bonds judgment was a significant assertion that the voter has a right to know who funds the parties competing for their vote. Two years on, the practical situation is that the instrument is gone and the demand it served has not disappeared. Political competition in India is expensive and becoming more so; expenditure limits apply to candidates and not to parties; and money that cannot flow through a legal, disclosed channel does not stop flowing.

The honest position is that India has removed an opaque mechanism without building a transparent one, and that this state is less stable than either alternative. What a serious settlement requires is not complicated to describe: real-time disclosure of contributions above a modest threshold, party-level expenditure limits with audited returns, and an enforcement body with the capacity and independence to act on what it finds. What it requires politically is a governing party and an opposition willing to constrain themselves simultaneously, which is why it has not happened.

What this Review believes

Since this is the first essay, it is worth stating the position it is written from.

Politics is the promise. Governance is the delivery. India argues intensely about who should hold power and comparatively little about whether power, once held, reaches the citizen. The electoral machinery described above is the clearest evidence available that Indian administration can achieve extraordinary things when a task is defined precisely, owned by a capable institution, and measured honestly. The Election Commission does not do the impossible because Indians are better at elections than at electricity or schooling. It does it because the task has an unambiguous deadline, a single accountable authority, and a result that cannot be fudged.

That is the argument this Review will make repeatedly, in different sectors, with different numbers: India's delivery failures are not failures of ambition, intelligence or resources. They are failures of institutional design — of tasks without owners, targets without deadlines, and results that nobody is required to publish.

The counter-case, honestly stated

Three arguments cut against this analysis, and the first cuts against the Review's founding claim.

First, the election may not generalise. The lesson drawn here is that the Commission succeeds because the task has an unambiguous deadline, a single accountable authority and a result that cannot be fudged — and that India's other failures are therefore failures of institutional design. But an election is unusual in ways that do not transfer. It is a single simultaneous event of short duration with a binary output, staffed by officers seconded from other departments who return to them immediately, and conducted under continuous adversarial surveillance by competing parties who possess both the means and the motive to detect and publicise any failure. Schooling, electricity and public health are continuous services with contested quality measures and no adversarial monitor with a stake in exposing shortfalls. The Commission's design is admirable. The claim that its lesson can be lifted into sectors that lack an adversarial audit mechanism is the weakest joint in the argument this Review is built on, and it is more honest to name that here than to leave it implicit across thirty essays.

Second, publishing a representative's record may produce the behaviour it measures rather than the behaviour it wants. Attendance, questions asked and debate participation are countable and only loosely related to whether a legislator is useful; a member who files many questions may achieve less than one who resolves constituents' problems without generating a parliamentary record. This Review's own argument elsewhere is that systems reliably optimise for whatever they count — that is the whole case against certificate-counting in skilling and compliance-reporting in district administration. A published legislative scorecard invites exactly that distortion, and the essay recommends it without applying its own warning.

Third, the reading of simultaneous elections is less balanced than it appears. India's staggered calendar means some consequential election is nearly always imminent, and the resulting permanent campaign has its own documented costs: hard decisions deferred past the next state contest, and expenditure timed to it. Fewer accountability moments with higher stakes and higher attention may deliver more genuine scrutiny than a continuous cycle in which every difficult decision waits. The essay grants that these arguments are respectable and then treats the net effect on the citizen's leverage as self-evidently negative.

The first recommendation survives all of this and remains the most valuable thing on the list. Roll revision on a fixed statutory calendar, away from election dates, with additions and deletions published in full and a real appeal for any citizen removed, is defensible whatever one concludes about the rest.

What we would do

  1. Put electoral roll revision on a fixed statutory calendar. Away from election dates, with full publication of additions and deletions and a time-bound appeal for any citizen removed. Most disputes about rolls are really disputes about timing and opacity.
  2. Publish a representative's record as a matter of course. Attendance, questions, debate participation, and constituency funds sanctioned and completed — per member, per year, in a standard format. The data largely exists; it is simply not assembled where a voter would find it.
  3. Settle the financing architecture before the next general election, not after. Disclosure thresholds, party expenditure limits, audited returns, and an enforcement capability. The current vacuum benefits incumbents of every colour, which is precisely why it persists.
  4. Pair any change to the electoral calendar with a strengthening of interval accountability. If elections are to be less frequent, the years between them must carry more scrutiny, not less.
  5. Protect the Commission's independence as a first-order concern. The most valuable institutional asset India possesses in this domain is a referee that both sides have historically accepted. That acceptance is easier to lose than to rebuild, and nothing in the reform agenda is worth spending it on.

India has built the most impressive voting machinery in the world and surrounded it with institutions that have not been rebuilt in decades. The vote is collected superbly. What it purchases — representation that reflects the present population, a legislature accountable between contests, and competition financed in the open — is where the work now is.

That gap, between the act and what it buys, is the subject of this journal.
