Fifty-Eight Thousand, Eight Hundred and Eighty-One — Manipur’s Displaced and the Border Policy Above Them

Pritiraj BrahmaFounding Partner, Pritiraj & Partners · 19 August 2026

Manipur’s Home Department puts the displaced at 58,881 and the relief camps still open at 174. Above them, India has scrapped a working border rule and replaced it with a fence less than two per cent built and a pass system two neighbouring states administer differently.

ProvenanceDisplacement and relief-camp figures are from a Right to Information reply by the Manipur Home Department reported in April 2026, current to 30 March 2026 and 10 March 2026 respectively. Border fencing figures are as stated by the Ministry of Home Affairs and reported through 2025-26. Death-toll figures differ materially across official and reported sources and are given as a range.

Three years and three months after 3 May 2023, the Manipur Home Department’s own answer to a Right to Information query put the displaced at 58,881 and the relief camps still operating at 174. Ten weeks before that answer, the state had announced it would resettle more than 40,000 of them by 31 March 2026 — roughly four times what the preceding three years had achieved, in one quarter.

India’s eastern frontier is where two kinds of state failure meet. One is a security architecture being rebuilt around a fence. The other is a population being administered as a camp roll. They are the same failure of institutional design, and they are usually written about separately.

President’s Rule was imposed in Manipur on 13 February 2025 and ran until 3 February 2026; an elected government under Chief Minister Yumnam Khemchand Singh took office on 4 February 2026. Across that transition, the numbers below barely moved.

Five findings anchor this analysis:

  1. The displacement figure has not materially fallen in three years, and the state’s own records say so. The Manipur Home Department, replying to a Right to Information query by the Congress leader Hareshwar Goshwami and reported in April 2026, put displacement from 3 May 2023 to 30 March 2026 at 58,881 people, with 174 relief camps operational as of 10 March 2026. More than 300 camps were originally set up, sheltering close to 60,000 people. The camp count has halved. The displaced count has not.
  2. Resettlement is running at roughly a sixth of the announced target, and the target keeps moving. In January 2026 the Chief Secretary announced resettlement of more than 10,000 families comprising over 40,000 people by 31 March 2026. Around the same date the state reported about 10,000 people from more than 2,200 families actually resettled, with ₹124 crore released. Camp closure had earlier been promised by December 2025 under a three-phase plan. Three deadlines have now been announced and none met — and each new one was announced without an account of why the last was missed.
  3. The money has been sanctioned; the resettlement has not happened. The Ministry of Home Affairs approved ₹424.36 crore for relief camp operations and ₹523 crore for rehabilitation of internally displaced persons — ₹947 crore in total — with a further ₹734 crore allocated in the 2026-27 state Budget, and 3,000 pre-fabricated houses built by the Manipur Police Housing Corporation. This is the pattern the Review keeps finding: the fiscal step completes and the delivery step does not. Money sanctioned is not a family returned to a village.
  4. India replaced a working border rule with an instrument it cannot finish this decade. The Free Movement Regime, which allowed hill-tribe residents on both sides of the 1,643-kilometre India-Myanmar border to cross within a defined radius, was scrapped in February 2024. In its place, the Cabinet Committee on Security gave in-principle approval in September 2024 for fencing and a patrol road along the whole border at an estimated ₹31,000 crore over ten years. As of 2025, about 30 kilometres were physically fenced — under 2 per cent — including a 10.023-kilometre stretch at Moreh awarded to the Border Roads Organisation, plus two one-kilometre hybrid surveillance pilots in Arunachal Pradesh and Manipur. The BRO’s own timeline runs to 2035-36. By comparison the Pakistan and Bangladesh border fences are each over 60 per cent complete. The rule was withdrawn in 2024; its replacement is scheduled to exist in 2036.
  5. The successor pass system is administered differently by neighbouring states, which means it is not one policy. From December 2024, Ministry of Home Affairs rules required QR-coded biometric border passes issued by the Assam Rifles, valid for a week and limited to within 10 kilometres of the boundary. In practice Mizoram’s government declined to biometrically register Myanmar refugees and continued to provide them services, while Manipur deported hundreds in late 2024. Of the 1,643-kilometre border — Arunachal Pradesh 520 km, Mizoram 510 km, Manipur 398 km, Nagaland 215 km — 1,472 km has been demarcated. A single national border regime that two states in the same region implement in opposite directions is a regime on paper only.

Where the sources disagree

The death toll from the violence is reported at 217 in one account of the same period, at 250 in a mid-2025 report, and at more than 260 in reporting from early 2026. We do not reconcile these. No consolidated, dated, official casualty statement appears to be public, and an essay that picked one figure and presented it as settled would be misrepresenting the state of the record. That absence is itself a finding: three years on, the number of people killed in an internal conflict inside India is not a published, revisable, official series.

The displacement figures show the same problem in smaller form. 58,881 is the Home Department’s own number to 30 March 2026; “over 60,000” and “nearly 59,000” both circulate; a further 12,611 people had crossed into Mizoram in the early weeks of the conflict, sheltering in 38 camps there, and it is not clear from public records whether they are inside or outside the Manipur count.

The camp as an institution

A relief camp is designed to be temporary. Its administration assumes a short horizon: shared shelter, ration distribution, no tenure, no address, no school catchment, no local body. When it lasts three years, every one of those assumptions becomes a defect. The camp becomes a semi-permanent settlement without any of the entitlements a settlement carries.

Two things follow. First, residents lose the ordinary machinery of citizenship — the ration card tied to a home district, the school seat, the health sub-centre, the electoral roll entry at a real address. Second, and worse for policy, the population becomes measurable only as a camp roll. Once a family is a camp roll entry, the state’s success measure becomes camp closure rather than return. The camp count fell from over 300 to 174 while the displaced count stayed near 59,000. That is the metric substituting itself for the outcome, in the open.

The counter-case, honestly stated

The strongest defence of the Union government’s record here is that lethal violence has fallen sharply. Ministers told Parliament in August 2025 that only one violent death had occurred since the imposition of President’s Rule and none in the preceding four months, and the government has held since early 2025 that large-scale violence had ceased. If the first duty is to stop the killing, that duty has substantially been discharged, and no honest account of Manipur can skip it.

On resettlement, the state’s caution is also defensible on its own terms. Kuki-Zo displaced persons have in large numbers declined to return to the valley, and many press instead for a separate administration — a demand Meitei organisations reject outright. Forcing returns into that would be worse than delay. A government that resettles slowly because it will not resettle people into danger is making a defensible choice, and camp closure targets announced without security guarantees would be the actual failure.

On the border, the case for the fence is that the Free Movement Regime was being used by insurgent groups and narcotics networks operating across a boundary where large stretches are effectively controlled by ethnic armed organisations amid Myanmar’s civil war. That is a real security problem and the FMR did not address it.

All of that can be true while the sequencing remains indefensible: the permission was withdrawn immediately and the substitute is twelve years out.

What we would do

  1. Publish one monthly displacement and return statement for Manipur. Displaced, in camps, resettled to original village, resettled elsewhere, and still to be resettled — five columns, one date, one issuing authority, published monthly, including those sheltering in Mizoram. The state answered these questions to an RTI applicant; there is no reason a citizen should need an RTI to obtain them.
  2. Stop measuring camp closure and start measuring return. The published target should be families returned to a place of their choosing with tenure and security, not camps shut. The two diverge exactly where the policy is weakest, and only one of them is what a displaced family wants.
  3. Publish an official, dated, revisable casualty record. One number, from one authority, with a stated method and a revision history. A country that cannot state how many of its citizens died in a three-year internal conflict cannot discuss it seriously — and the vacuum is filled by whichever figure suits the speaker.
  4. Sequence the border regime honestly: publish the fence schedule kilometre by kilometre, and hold an interim movement rule until it is built. If the fence completes in 2035-36, then a documented, biometric, uniformly administered crossing arrangement is required until 2035-36 — not as a concession but as the operative regime. Withdrawing the rule a decade before its replacement exists creates the informality it was meant to end.
  5. Make the pass regime uniform, or admit it is not national. One issuing protocol, one radius, one validity, applied identically in Mizoram, Manipur, Nagaland and Arunachal Pradesh, with published issuance and refusal counts by sector. Where a state government cannot implement it, that disagreement belongs in the open and in the Inter-State Council — not in divergent practice on the ground.

The Review’s position is that politics is the promise and governance is the delivery. On India’s eastern frontier the promises are a secure border and the return of the displaced. The delivery is 2 per cent of a fence, three missed resettlement deadlines, and a citizen having to file a Right to Information request to learn how many of his neighbours are still living in a camp.
