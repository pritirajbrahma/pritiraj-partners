The Half That Does Not Show Up — India's Female Participation Problem

Pritiraj BrahmaFounding Partner, Pritiraj & Partners · 5 March 2026

Female labour force participation has reached 40 per cent, urban female participation sits at 25.3 per cent, and 44.4 per cent of women outside the workforce name care work as the reason. The Time Use Survey puts a figure on it: 289 minutes a day of unpaid domestic work against 88 for men — a gap that closed by one minute in five years. The constraint is not ambition.

ProvenancePublished 5 March 2026; revised 28 August 2026 to add a sixth finding, the time-use comparison and a fuller counter-case. Participation and unemployment figures are from the Periodic Labour Force Survey Annual Report 2025 and the December 2025 monthly bulletin. Time-use figures are from the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation Time Use Survey conducted January to December 2024 and released February 2025, against the 2019 round; published accounts of the same survey give women’s unpaid time as 236, 289 or 305 minutes on three different definitional bases, and all three are noted rather than reconciled. The labour force survey and the time use survey measure different things and are not a single series.

India’s female participation number has improved substantially and still describes a workforce most women do not enter. The reason they give is care: 44.4 per cent of women outside the labour force name child care and home-making. The Time Use Survey puts a figure on what that costs — 289 minutes a day of unpaid domestic work against 88 for men, a gap that closed by exactly one minute in five years.

The Periodic Labour Force Survey Annual Report for 2025 puts the labour force participation rate for persons aged 15 and above at 59.3 per cent — 79.1 per cent for men and 40.0 per cent for women. The worker population ratio stands at 57.4 per cent overall, 76.6 per cent for men and 38.8 per cent for women.

Forty per cent is a genuine improvement on the sub-25 per cent figures of the late 2010s, and the improvement should be credited. It also leaves India with one of the widest participation gaps among large economies, and the composition of the improvement matters as much as its size.

Six findings anchor this analysis:

  1. The stated reason is care, and it is stated overwhelmingly. Among men outside the labour force, 69.8 per cent cite continuing education. Among women, 44.4 per cent cite child care and personal commitments in home-making. Two populations, two entirely different constraints, one undifferentiated policy response.
  2. The gain is rural, and the urban number has barely moved. In December 2025, female participation stood at 40.1 per cent in rural areas against 25.3 per cent in urban areas; rural female worker population ratio was 38.6 per cent against an urban figure of about 23.0 per cent. India’s cities — where formal jobs, transport and childcare markets exist — perform worse than its villages.
  3. The unpaid-work gap has not narrowed. The Time Use Survey conducted over January to December 2024 and released in February 2025 records women aged 15 to 59 spending 289 minutes a day on unpaid domestic services for household members against 88 minutes for men. Five years earlier the figures were 299 and 97. Women’s time fell by ten minutes; men’s by nine. On caregiving, 41 per cent of women in that band participated on a given day against 21.4 per cent of men, at 140 minutes against 74.
  4. Educated young urban women face the highest unemployment of any group. Urban young women record an unemployment rate of 18.9 per cent against 11.8 per cent for urban young men. These are women who have entered the labour force and cannot find work — a different failure from non-participation, and one that discourages the next cohort from entering at all.
  5. The two instruments disagree, and the disagreement is the finding. The Time Use Survey records 25 per cent of women aged 15 to 59 in employment-related activity on the day surveyed, against 75 per cent of men, up from 21.8 per cent in 2019. That sits well below the 40 per cent participation rate. One counts attachment to the labour force over a reference period; the other counts work actually performed on a day. Fifteen percentage points separate them.
  6. Much rural participation is self-employment and unpaid family labour. Across the workforce the self-employed share is around 56 per cent and regular wage or salaried work about 24 per cent. Rural female participation rises substantially through work on family holdings, which counts as participation and does not necessarily deliver independent income.

What the day actually contains

The participation series records whether a woman is in the labour force. It cannot record what she is doing instead, which is why the constraint is better established by a different instrument. When 44.4 per cent of women outside the labour force cite child care and home-making, the Time Use Survey shows what that answer costs in hours.

A woman carrying nearly five hours of daily unpaid household work does not have a labour supply problem that childcare policy might marginally improve; she has a full working day already allocated. At entry-level urban wages, purchasing replacement care for that block of time is not affordable. The decision not to work is arithmetic rather than attitude — and that makes it a supply-side infrastructure question rather than a question of aspiration.

Why the urban number is the diagnostic one

If the barrier to women’s work were cultural preference alone, urban participation should exceed rural — cities are less traditional, better educated and richer. India shows the opposite. That inverted pattern is the strongest available evidence that the binding constraint is practical rather than attitudinal.

Three practical constraints dominate, and each has a named owner in government. Care is scarce, expensive and mostly unregulated; in a rural joint household it is absorbed informally, in a nuclear urban household it must be purchased. Safety and transport impose a commute risk that falls disproportionately on women and functions as a tax on the wage; the effect appears in evaluations of free bus travel schemes in several states, where ridership and reported work-seeking both rose. Job structure assumes continuous full-time attendance with no accommodation for a career interrupted by care, so a woman who exits at childbirth finds re-entry priced as though her prior experience did not exist.

The counter-case, honestly stated

Three objections deserve a hearing, and the third is against our own remedy.

First, some of the measured increase reflects better measurement rather than more work. The revamped survey design, with monthly bulletins and a calendar-year cycle from 2025, captures activity that earlier rounds classified as domestic duty. If so, part of the improvement is statistical. That cuts both ways and honestly stated it strengthens the case: if women were always working and India was failing to count it, the policy failure is older and larger than the participation series suggests.

Second, the rural-urban inversion may be partly definitional rather than wholly diagnostic. Rural participation rises through work on family holdings, which the survey counts and which has no urban equivalent; a rural woman assisting on a plot and an urban woman looking after a household may be doing comparable unpaid work and being counted differently. That would mean the inversion overstates how much better villages are at employing women, while leaving the practical-constraint argument intact for the urban figure itself — 25.3 per cent is low against any comparator, definitional or not.

Third, and most seriously: treating childcare as labour-supply infrastructure is a proposition, not an established finding. The evidence that converting anganwadis to full-day provision raises female employment in India specifically is thin — the international evidence is stronger than the domestic evidence, and the domestic pilots are small. Our recommendation therefore has to be framed as a measured experiment with a published outcome, not as a known solution. An essay that criticises undifferentiated policy responses should not propose one.

What we would do

  1. Treat childcare as economic infrastructure, and evaluate it as such. Not as a welfare scheme for children but as a labour-supply intervention for mothers, with female employment as the reported outcome. Anganwadi infrastructure exists in almost every village and could carry a full-day component with staffing and hours designed around a working day. Because the domestic evidence is thin, do it in twenty districts first and publish the employment result against twenty matched controls.
  2. Set an explicit urban target with a named owner. Urban female participation near 25 per cent is the specific failure. It should be a published state target with a department accountable for it, not a line in an aggregate.
  3. Make transport safety a measured output. Lit stops, last-mile connectivity, staffed interchanges — reported per corridor, with women’s ridership as the outcome measure rather than kilometres built.
  4. Legislate re-entry. Returnship provisions in public employment and public procurement conditions, so that a career gap for care is not treated as a competence gap. This is the cheapest of these measures and the one with the clearest mechanism.
  5. Publish the two measures side by side, and separate unpaid family labour in reporting. The 40 per cent and the 25 per cent should appear in the same table with their definitions stated, and participation that generates no independent income should be reported separately from participation that does. Reporting them together flatters the number and obscures the policy question.

India’s demographic dividend arithmetic does not work with 40 per cent of its women in the labour force, a quarter of them in its cities, and a fifteen-point gap between women attached to the labour force and women who did paid work yesterday. No country has converted a demographic window while leaving half its working-age population outside the economy — and India’s own data now names the reason, in the words of the women themselves and in the minutes of their day.
