The Unemployment Families Absorb — Why India’s Graduate Jobless Rate Stays Out of Sight

Pritiraj BrahmaFounding Partner, Pritiraj & Partners · 2 October 2026

India’s unemployment rate was 3.2 per cent in 2024. Among graduates it was 13 per cent, and among the illiterate close to zero. That gap is not a paradox: to be counted as unemployed, a person has to be able to afford the wait. Graduates’ families pay for it, and no public statistic records what they pay. That is why India’s most educated unemployment is also its least visible.

India’s unemployment rate was 3.2 per cent in 2024. Among its graduates it was 13 per cent, and among the illiterate it was close to zero, on Data For India’s analysis of the Periodic Labour Force Survey. That gap is not a paradox. In the survey’s terms, unemployment is something a person has to be able to afford: having no work, and continuing to look for the right work rather than taking any work. The households that can pay for that wait are mostly the households whose children hold degrees. India’s graduate unemployment is real, and most of its cost is carried by parents, not by any public instrument. That is why it so rarely shows up anywhere except the dinner table and the examination hall.

The headline unemployment rate is among the most quoted numbers in Indian politics and among the least informative. It is low because most Indians cannot afford to be unemployed. A person with no savings and no family support takes whatever work exists, whether daily wage labour, a share of the family farm or a few hours of help in a relative’s shop, and is counted as employed. The person who can wait for a job that matches a degree is counted as unemployed. The rate therefore measures, in part, who has someone paying for them to keep looking.

Six findings anchor this analysis:

  1. Joblessness is concentrated at the top of the education ladder. Data For India’s analysis of PLFS microdata puts unemployment at 3.2 per cent overall in 2024, 13 per cent among graduates and nearly zero among the illiterate. The ILO–IHD India Employment Report 2024 found the same gradient among young people in 2022: 29.1 per cent unemployment for graduates against 3.4 per cent for those who cannot read or write. A press extraction from the PLFS calendar-2025 annual series puts all-age graduate unemployment at 11.2 per cent. The years and age bands differ; the direction does not.
  2. It is overwhelmingly a problem of the first years after graduation. Data For India finds more than a third of graduates in their early twenties unemployed, and 85 per cent of all unemployed graduates under the age of 30. The rate falls steadily with age. The State of Working India 2026, as reported, puts unemployment above 40 per cent for graduates under 25, against around 21 per cent for those whose highest qualification is higher secondary.
  3. Graduates now make up most of India’s young unemployed. The State of Working India 2026 reports that graduates were 67 per cent of unemployed Indians aged 20 to 29 in 2023, up from 46 per cent in 2017. India had 6.3 crore graduates in that age band, up from 50 lakh in 1983, and roughly 1.1 crore of them, close to 17 per cent, were unemployed in 2023.
  4. Supply has outrun absorption for two decades. Between 2004-05 and 2023, the same report finds, India added roughly 50 lakh graduates a year, while only around 28 lakh a year found employment. The gap is not a single bad year. It is a structural surplus that has been building for a generation.
  5. The cost of waiting falls on households, and no statistic records it. The ILO–IHD report attributes educated unemployment both to a shortage of quality jobs and to the ability of educated young people to hold out for better ones. That ability is family money: rent, food, coaching fees and examination fees for a graduate with no income. The PLFS does not ask who supports an unemployed member, for how long, or at what cost. India has no national unemployment allowance for first-time job seekers.
  6. The burden is heaviest for women and in particular states. The PLFS calendar-2025 extraction puts graduate unemployment at 17.4 per cent for women against 9.0 per cent for men. Reporting from the State of Working India 2026 places unemployment among male graduates under 30 between 8.8 per cent in Delhi and 40.3 per cent in Odisha, and among female graduates as high as 73.5 per cent in Jammu and Kashmir. A national rate hides a set of very different state labour markets.

Why the poorest are almost never unemployed

The PLFS classifies a person by usual status over the reference year. Someone who worked a relatively long part of the year, or at least 30 days in a subsidiary capacity, is counted as employed. For a young person from a poor household this is almost automatic. Work of some kind is found, because it has to be. The result is a near-zero unemployment rate at the bottom of the education ladder, which says nothing about the quality, security or pay of the work being done.

The graduate faces a different calculation. The jobs that match the degree are few, the jobs that do not match it pay little, and taking one can feel like closing the door on the other. If the family can carry them, they wait. The survey records the wait as unemployment. It does not record the household that is paying for it.

What the household is paying for

The family’s contribution is the hidden half of the statistic. It pays for months or years of search, very often spent preparing for a government recruitment examination. The scale of that queue is visible in the examination record: the Indian Express counted 1.4 crore applicants for a little over 1.04 lakh posts in the recruitment examinations disrupted by leaks over five years. Every applicant in that queue is, in effect, being financed by someone. When a paper leaks and an examination is cancelled, the household pays again.

This is a private insurance system, and like most private insurance it is unequal. Graduates from families who can afford to wait search for longer and, eventually, find better-matched work. Graduates from families who cannot take what exists, disappear from the unemployment figures, and appear instead in the statistics on informal, low-paid work. The headline rate is lower because of them, not better.

Why it stays out of sight

Unemployment that is financed privately is also absorbed privately. It does not show up as a claim on a public scheme, because there is no national scheme to claim from. It does not show up in the formal payroll data, because the person was never on a payroll. It shows up at home as dependence and delay: later marriage, later independence, years of preparation for one more attempt. Politically it surfaces in only one place, the examination hall, which is why, as the companion essay argues, the largest student protests in India are about recruitment examinations.

Some states have begun to make the cost visible by paying part of it. Karnataka’s Yuva Nidhi scheme pays a monthly allowance to unemployed graduates and diploma holders for a limited period after they qualify. Whatever one thinks of such allowances as policy, they have one useful effect: they produce a register of who is waiting, for how long, and what happens to them. The national statistical system has nothing equivalent.

The counter-case

There is a serious argument that this is not a crisis. Search is rational. A graduate who waits for a better-matched job may earn more over a lifetime than one who takes the first offer. Data For India’s figures show the graduate unemployment rate falling steadily with age, which suggests most graduates do eventually find work. On this view, high unemployment among 22-year-olds is a transition, not a trap, and families supporting the transition are making a sensible investment.

Part of that is correct. The fall with age is real, and not every month of search is wasted. But the State of Working India’s two-decade gap between graduates added and graduates employed is not a transition that clears. It is a surplus that accumulates. And an investment that only some families can afford is not a labour-market policy; it is a sorting mechanism by household income. The argument for the wait is strongest for the graduates who need help least.

What we would do

  1. Publish graduate unemployment as a headline series. Every quarterly PLFS bulletin should carry the unemployment rate by education level and age band, including graduates aged 20 to 29, alongside the headline rate. A number that matters this much to this many households should not have to be extracted from microdata by independent analysts.
  2. Add a household-support module to the PLFS. For each unemployed member, ask who supports them, for how many months they have been searching, and what the household spends on coaching and examination fees. That would turn the hidden half of the statistic into a measured one.
  3. Track graduate cohorts into payroll. Link AISHE graduation records to EPFO payroll data, with privacy protections, and publish the share of each graduating cohort on a formal payroll one and three years after graduation, by discipline and by state.
  4. Shorten the public recruitment queue the state itself creates. Publish a binding annual recruitment calendar for every state and Union recruitment body, with results deadlines. A large part of the graduate wait is time spent in a queue whose timing the state controls.
  5. Evaluate state unemployment allowances on outcomes, and publish the results. Where states such as Karnataka pay allowances to unemployed graduates, require a published evaluation of who claimed, for how long, and how many moved into work. These schemes are the only place the wait is being measured; their data should inform national policy.

A country that counts unemployment only when someone can afford it will keep reading its lowest rates as its best ones. Politics is the promise. Governance is the delivery. A nation is judged not by its headline rate, but by whether the graduate in its last home is waiting for a job or waiting for the state to notice they are waiting.
