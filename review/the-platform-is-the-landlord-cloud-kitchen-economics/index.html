The Platform Is the Landlord — Food Delivery, Commissions and the Economics of India’s Cloud Kitchens

Pritiraj BrahmaFounding Partner, Pritiraj & Partners · 24 November 2026

Restaurants report paying food delivery platforms 10 to 30 per cent in commission, and up to half of what the customer paid once ads, discounts and fees are added. Two platforms hold about 90 per cent of the market. For a delivery kitchen, the rent has moved from the street to the app.

ProvenancePublished 24 November 2026. Commission and fee figures are as reported by trade and business publications between June and September 2025, citing operators, advisers and industry bodies; platforms do not publish a standard rate card, and the ranges reported differ. They are labelled as reporting throughout. Disclosure: the author is the founder of East & Ember Hospitality Group, a delivery-first food company.

A restaurant used to pay its landlord for footfall. A delivery kitchen pays the platform instead, and the platform sets the rent.

India’s food delivery market made a new kind of restaurant possible: a kitchen with no dining room, in a low-rent unit, serving customers it never meets. The model is now mainstream. This essay asks what that kitchen actually pays for its customers, and why that number decides whether the business is worth backing.

Four findings anchor this analysis:

  1. The commission is a range, not a rate. Reported commissions run from 10 to 28 per cent on Zomato (NDTV Profit, reported by Verdict Food Service, September 2025), 10 to 16 per cent for larger brands and above 20 per cent for smaller outlets (an Alvarez & Marsal partner quoted by YourStory, July 2025), and 16 to 30 per cent across both major platforms (Storyboard18, June 2025). Terms vary with order value, volume and brand. The small operator pays the most.
  2. The commission is not the whole take. Operators interviewed by YourStory described commission, advertising and discovery fees together taking 45 to 50 per cent of payouts. A restaurant-software founder quoted by Inc42 in August 2025 put the figure at 35 to 40 per cent of each transaction once logistics, payment and ad charges are counted. Visibility is increasingly bought, not earned.
  3. The market is concentrated, and it is being contested. Swiggy and Zomato together hold about 90 per cent of food delivery (Inc42, August 2025). Rapido’s entry offered restaurants a fixed fee of ₹25 on orders below ₹400 and ₹50 above (Storyboard18, June 2025), and Inc42 reported a government plan to commit ₹200 to 250 crore to support food delivery on ONDC.
  4. The platform also competes with its tenants. The National Restaurant Association of India, which represents over five lakh restaurants, has moved the Competition Commission over platforms’ own private-label food brands and their control of customer data (Inc42, August 2025).

Why the platform is the landlord

In a dine-in restaurant, rent buys location and location buys demand. A delivery kitchen sits in a cheap unit, and its demand comes from its ranking on two apps. The commission and the advertising budget are therefore its real occupancy cost. Unlike a lease, they are variable, opaque and set by one side.

That changes what makes a delivery business investable. The useful question is not gross order value but contribution after platform costs, and how much of the customer relationship the kitchen owns. Repeat customers, a direct ordering channel and food that travels well are the delivery equivalent of a long lease.

The counter-case, honestly stated

The platforms built this market. They pay for riders, the app and the customer acquisition no single restaurant could afford, and a 20 per cent commission is cheaper than a high-street lease in most Indian cities. Restaurants that complain about commission are often complaining about competition, and kitchens that exist only because of delivery apps have little standing to object to their price.

Much of that is true, and it is why delivery-first kitchens exist at all. But a market in which the supplier cannot see its own customer, cannot predict its own ranking and competes with the platform’s own brands is not yet a fair one. The remedy is transparency, not a price cap.

What we would do

  1. Publish the full take on every order. One statement per order showing commission, advertising, discount share and fees.
  2. Give restaurants their repeat-customer data. At minimum, an identifier that lets a kitchen recognise and reward its own regulars.
  3. Separate the marketplace from the private label. Where a platform runs its own food brands, publish and audit the ranking rules.
  4. Fund ONDC delivery, not just onboarding. A third channel is real only when it can deliver the food reliably.
  5. For operators: price for the platform, build for the direct channel. Cost every dish at full platform take, and invest in the customer who will one day order direct.

The delivery economy has made it cheaper than ever to open a kitchen and harder than ever to own a customer. The businesses that last will be the ones that treat the platform as a landlord to be managed, not a partner to be trusted. Politics is the promise. Governance is the delivery.
