The Country That Stopped Counting Itself

Pritiraj BrahmaFounding Partner, Pritiraj & Partners · 12 February 2026

India's last census was in 2011. Every welfare list, every seat allocation and every per-capita figure in public use rests on a population that no longer exists.

ProvenancePublished 12 February 2026. Last substantively revised 10 August 2026. Most recent completed census: 2011. The 2021 census has not been conducted.

A state that cannot count its people cannot target them, fund them, or represent them accurately. India has governed for fifteen years on a photograph of itself taken in 2011.

Almost every important number in Indian public life is a fraction. Per-capita income, doctors per lakh, police per lakh, seats per state, the share of a population entitled to subsidised food — each has a population figure in its denominator. When that denominator is stale, every fraction built on it is wrong in the same direction, and wrong by a growing margin.

India's last completed census was in 2011. The 2021 round was postponed; enumeration is now scheduled with house-listing in 2026, the population count in February 2027, and a reference date of 1 March 2027. The Law Minister has told Parliament that results are not expected before late 2027 or early 2028. By the time India next knows its own size with precision, it will have gone roughly seventeen years without.

Four findings anchor this analysis:

  1. Entitlement lists are capped on 2011 numbers. The National Food Security Act, 2013 fixes coverage at 75 per cent of the rural and 50 per cent of the urban population, determined from the last published census. Population has grown since; the covered share has not been recalculated. The exclusion this produces is not a targeting error — it is arithmetic operating exactly as drafted.
  2. Representation is frozen on 1971, and redistribution will be computed on a base that is itself contested. Lok Sabha seat allocation still rests on the 1971 census. The delimitation now proposed must work from either 2011 figures, projections, or administrative registers, because the 2027 count will not be ready in time.
  3. The gap is filled by projection, not measurement. The Technical Group on Population Projections produces the estimates most official documents now rely on. Projections are sound in aggregate and unreliable at the district level — which is the level at which schools are sited, PHCs staffed and constituencies drawn.
  4. Survey confidence has been contested in the same period. The withheld 2017-18 consumption expenditure survey, the revision of GDP back-series, and the shift of the Periodic Labour Force Survey to a calendar-year cycle with monthly bulletins have each drawn methodological argument. Some of that argument is partisan; some of it is serious. The distinction matters, and a stronger statistical system is what allows the public to tell them apart.

Why a delayed census is not a delayed report

A census is not a document. It is the frame from which nearly every other Indian survey draws its sample. The Periodic Labour Force Survey, the National Family Health Survey, consumption surveys — all use census-derived sampling frames. A frame that is fifteen years old systematically under-represents exactly what has changed most: new urban settlements, peri-urban growth, migrant-dense districts.

The consequence is subtle and compounding. Surveys do not simply become less precise; they become biased toward the India that existed when the frame was drawn. Every downstream estimate inherits that bias, and no amount of sample size corrects it.

The exclusion, counted three ways

The clearest measurable consequence of a stale denominator is in food security, and the estimates of its size are worth setting out together, because their disagreement is itself the finding.

The National Food Security Act determines coverage as a share of the population taken from the last published census, producing a national ceiling of 81.35 crore beneficiaries computed on Census 2011. How many people the growth since then has left outside that ceiling depends on who is counting. The economists Jean Drèze and Reetika Khera put the number excluded at roughly ten crore. Other independent researchers have put it nearer thirteen crore. The Union government's own affidavit to the Supreme Court in August 2023, derived from matching e-Shram registrations against food distribution records, identified approximately eight crore people holding no ration card.

Eight crore, ten crore, thirteen crore. This Review does not propose to adjudicate between them, and the range is not the product of carelessness — it reflects genuinely different questions about who is eligible, who is registered and who is in need. The point is narrower and harder to argue with: no official figure exists, because establishing one requires the enumeration whose absence created the problem. A state that cannot count its people also cannot count the people its own statute has excluded, and the size of the exclusion is therefore contested in a range of five crore human beings.

The legal history matters as much as the arithmetic. The Supreme Court has directed the Union to devise a formula under which entitlements are not restricted to 2011 census figures. The Union's position has been that broadening coverage would require amending the Act. Both positions are defensible on their own terms, and between them sits the structural fact this essay is about: the statute delegated a policy question to a dataset, and the dataset stopped being produced. Nobody decided to exclude ten crore people. Section 9 is operating exactly as drafted, against a census that has not arrived.

This is also the strongest available test of the administrative-register argument. The e-Shram cross-match worked; it found eight crore uncovered people, and it found them because each had registered with the state for something. It could not find, and cannot in principle find, the household that has registered for nothing. A register enumerates the population that has already made contact. A census exists to find the rest, and that is the function nothing else performs.

The counter-case, honestly stated

The government's position deserves a fair statement. A census of 1.4 billion people is among the largest peacetime operations any state attempts; the pandemic made 2021 impossible, and subsequent scheduling had to accommodate elections, enumerator availability and a shift to digital collection. The 2027 round is also being conducted with self-enumeration and mobile applications, which should improve both speed and accuracy. And administrative data — Aadhaar-linked registers, electoral rolls, PDS databases — genuinely does provide coverage that did not exist in 2011.

That last point is the strongest and also the most double-edged. Administrative registers count people who have transacted with the state. They are excellent for those inside the system and structurally blind to those outside it — which is precisely the population a census exists to find.

What we would do

  1. Publish the interim denominator, and defend it. Every ministry currently uses some population estimate. They should use the same one, it should be published, and its method should be open to challenge. At present different departments quietly use different bases.
  2. Uncap entitlement coverage from the last census. Where a statute fixes coverage as a share of population, the operative population should be the best current official estimate, not the last enumerated one. This is a small legislative fix with a very large number of people behind it.
  3. Publish the census timetable with milestones and hold to it. Enumeration dates, processing dates, release dates for each table. A census whose schedule is not public cannot be held to.
  4. Release micro-data on a fixed calendar, without ministerial discretion. The credibility damage from a withheld survey exceeds the political damage from a bad number. Statistical independence is cheap to grant and expensive to rebuild.
  5. Fund the state statistical machinery. Most states have a Directorate of Economics and Statistics operating at a fraction of sanctioned strength. National statistics are assembled from state work, and that is where the quality is actually determined.

A state's first competence is to know who it governs. India has spent fifteen years without that knowledge and has adapted to the absence rather than fixing it. Every delivery failure this Review examines is measured against a denominator nobody has verified since 2011.
