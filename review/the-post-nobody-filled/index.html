The Post Nobody Filled

Pritiraj BrahmaFounding Partner, Pritiraj & Partners · 23 August 2026

In India's 45 central universities, 1,529 of 2,553 sanctioned professor posts were vacant. Vacancy is worst at the senior end, worst again in reserved categories, and the ministry's position is that recruitment is not its responsibility. A sanctioned post is a budget line. A filled post is a teacher.

ProvenancePublished 23 August 2026. Post-wise and category-wise vacancy figures are from Lok Sabha replies of 12 December 2022, 20 March 2023, 25 November 2024 and 12 March 2025; institution-wide vacancy totals from the Rajya Sabha Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee report presented in March 2023. Two parliamentary replies weeks apart give different IIT vacancy figures; both are stated. No consolidated post-2024 institution-wise series is public.

Of 2,553 sanctioned professor posts across India's central universities, 1,529 were vacant — three in five. At associate professor level, 2,304 of 5,110. The government has filled posts since, in a declared mission-mode drive. It has not published an institution-wise series that would let anyone outside the ministry verify where the shortage now sits, and its formal position in Parliament is that faculty recruitment is a matter for each institution's board, with "no active role of the Ministry".

Every other constraint in Indian higher education is downstream of this one. Accreditation grades a teaching institution. Research output is produced by faculty. Employability is a claim about what a graduate was taught. A university with an empty chair is not a university with a temporary administrative problem; it is a university not delivering the thing it exists to deliver, to a cohort that will not get that year back.

Five findings anchor this analysis:

  1. Roughly a third of sanctioned teaching posts in central institutions were vacant, and the figure has been stable for years. Replying to the Lok Sabha on 12 December 2022, the Union education minister stated that of 18,956 sanctioned teaching posts in 45 central universities, 12,776 were filled and 6,180 vacant as on 1 December 2022 — 32.6 per cent. In the IITs, 4,502 of 11,170 sanctioned posts were vacant, 38.3 per cent; in the IIMs, 493 of 1,566. A separate reply weeks later, in February 2023, put IIT teaching vacancies at 4,425. The two official figures differ; we state both rather than choose.
  2. The shortage is concentrated at the senior end, where it does the most damage. On the same parliamentary record, 1,529 of 2,553 sanctioned professor posts were vacant (about 60 per cent), 2,304 of 5,110 associate professor posts (about 45 per cent), and 2,347 of 11,293 assistant professor posts (about 21 per cent). A system can absorb junior vacancy by loading teaching. It cannot absorb senior vacancy, because professors are what supervise doctoral students, chair selection committees, win research funding and hold departments to a standard. India's PhD enrolment is 1.8 lakh, 1.9 per cent of university enrolment. The people who would supervise that cohort are the posts most often empty.
  3. Reserved-category posts carry a distinct and larger backlog. Of 2,284 posts sanctioned for Scheduled Castes across the three teaching grades in central universities, 908 were unfilled; of 1,142 sanctioned for Scheduled Tribes, 544. A Lok Sabha reply of 20 March 2023 put total vacant reserved teaching posts across central universities at 3,874, with Banaras Hindu University (576) and the University of Delhi (526) highest. By 25 November 2024 the Minister of State for Education told the Lok Sabha that 5,182 teaching posts were vacant across central universities, of which 2,750 were reserved — 740 SC, 464 ST and 1,546 OBC.
  4. "None found suitable" is used as a disposal, and is barely documented. The March 2023 reply recorded 549 reserved teaching posts left unfilled over five years on the ground that no candidate was found suitable, across five central universities including Jawaharlal Nehru University and the University of Hyderabad. Whatever one concludes about any individual selection, a system that can decline to fill a reserved post 549 times without publishing the search record, the applicant count or the committee's reasoning has no mechanism by which that judgment can ever be tested.
  5. Recruitment accelerated; accountability for it was formally disclaimed. On 12 March 2025 the education minister told the Lok Sabha that 53,456 recruitments had been made in central universities, IITs and IIMs between May 2014 and December 2024, of which 10,857 faculty positions were filled between September 2022 and December 2024, including 1,347 SC, 579 ST and 2,219 OBC. Asked about de-reservation and category-wise vacancy, the minister replied that as autonomous institutions the bodies recruit under their own Acts and that recruitment powers vest with their Boards of Governors, with "no active role of the Ministry". The Rajya Sabha Standing Committee had earlier, in March 2023, recorded 34,099 vacancies across central higher education institutions with roughly 6,500 filled between July 2022 and February 2023 — under a fifth in a year — and asked for them to be filled by the end of that year.

Autonomy is not the same as unaccountability

The ministry's position is legally correct and institutionally incomplete. Central universities and the IITs are autonomous; their statutes vest appointment in their own boards. Autonomy of that kind is worth defending. But autonomy over the decision is not the same as immunity from the disclosure. The ministry funds these institutions, sanctions their posts, sets their reservation obligations through the Central Educational Institutions (Reservation in Teachers' Cadre) Act, 2019, and monitors them monthly by its own account. Nothing in autonomy prevents it from publishing, quarterly and institution-wise, the sanctioned, filled and vacant counts by grade and category. What is being disclaimed is not the power to appoint. It is the duty to publish.

The same gap shows in the IITs. An August 2025 parliamentary reply stated that IIT faculty posts are sanctioned on a 1:10 faculty-to-student ratio; a later reply recorded over 3,500 vacancies filled since 2022 and around 460 visiting faculty in the system. Neither disclosed the actual ratio at each IIT, nor a consolidated institute-wise breakdown of sanctioned, filled and vacant posts. The norm is public. Performance against the norm is not.

The scale of the underlying number

AISHE 2021-22 records 15.98 lakh teachers against a total enrolment of 4.33 crore — approximately 27 students per teacher across the system, against a norm of 1:10 in the institutions the state considers its best. Central institutions are a small fraction of this system; the vacancy data exists for them precisely because they are central. For state public universities, which carry around 31 per cent of university enrolment, and for the 45,473 colleges, no equivalent consolidated vacancy series is published at all. The part of the system we can see is the part performing best.

The counter-case

Three arguments cut against reading vacancy as failure, and each has force.

First, a sanctioned post is a budget line, not a classroom. Sanctions rise when institutions expand; a new IIT sanctions its full complement on day one and fills it over a decade. Part of the vacancy percentage is arithmetic produced by growth, and growth is good.

Second, the mission-mode drive did move real numbers: 15,637 faculty positions filled between September 2022 and December 2024, on the ministry's account, and around 3,500 in the IITs since 2022. A critique written from 2022 data and applied to 2026 would be unfair, which is precisely why the absence of a current published series matters to both sides of the argument.

Third, filling posts badly is worse than leaving them open. An institution that appoints to a professorship to clear a vacancy statistic has damaged itself for thirty years. "None found suitable" is capable of being an honest answer.

The response to all three is the same: none of them is an argument against publishing. Growth-driven vacancy, genuine recruitment progress and honest non-selection all survive disclosure. Only the fourth possibility — that posts are being left open for reasons nobody wishes to state — does not.

What we would do

  1. Publish a quarterly faculty return, institution-wise, in one fixed schema. Sanctioned, filled and vacant by grade and by category, for every central university, IIT, IIM, NIT and IISER, on a fixed date each quarter, in machine-readable form. This is a reporting instruction, not a statute, and it can be issued this year.
  2. Set a statutory filling deadline with a published reason for breach. A vacancy older than twelve months should require the institution's board to record, publicly, why the post is open and when it will be filled. The deadline is not the point; the required explanation is.
  3. Make "none found suitable" a reportable event. Where a selection committee declines to fill a reserved post, the institution should publish the number of applicants shortlisted, the number interviewed and the committee's stated ground — not the deliberations, not names, only the counts and the reason. Five hundred and forty-nine such decisions with no published record is not a defensible position for a public institution.
  4. Build the supply side deliberately, and count it. India enrols 1.8 lakh PhD students. Nobody publishes how many enter academic posts, in what discipline, or after how long. A national doctoral-to-faculty pipeline report, published annually by discipline, would show whether the shortage is of posts, of candidates, or of matching — three problems with three different remedies, currently indistinguishable.
  5. Put the vacancy metric in the Vice-Chancellor's terms of appointment. Where boards hold the recruitment power, the accountability must sit with the board. A performance agreement for the head of institution that names the vacancy position at entry and the target at exit costs nothing and places the duty where the authority already is.

India's higher education debate spends most of its energy on curriculum and regulation. Neither reaches a student except through a person standing in a room. Three in five professorships in the central universities were empty. That is the constraint, and unlike most constraints in this sector, it is one the state can lift with money it has already sanctioned.
