The Grievance With No Address — Why India’s Students Protest at the Examination Hall

Pritiraj BrahmaFounding Partner, Pritiraj & Partners · 2 October 2026

An Indian Express investigation counted 41 recruitment-exam leaks across 15 states in five years, derailing 1.4 crore applicants for about 1.04 lakh posts. Nine months after the UGC required every university to appoint a student ombudsperson, 421 had not. India’s largest student protests are not about ideology. They are about a state that runs the examination, sets the queue, and keeps no public record of either.

ProvenancePaper-leak counts are from investigative media tallies (The Indian Express, February 2024; India Today; Newslaundry; The Tribune, February 2024). They differ in period and method, and no official national series exists. Ombudsperson compliance figures are from UGC defaulter lists of January, March, June and July 2024, as reported. Graduate cohort figures are from the State of Working India 2026 (Azim Premji University).

India publishes no current national count of student protests, so the question of who protests has to be answered by what students protest about. On that the record is clear. The mobilisations that reach national scale are rarely about campus ideology. They are about examinations. An Indian Express investigation in February 2024 documented 41 leaks in recruitment examinations across 15 states in five years, governments of every party among them, derailing about 1.4 crore applicants for a little over 1.04 lakh posts. The state sets the paper, runs the hall, publishes the result and offers the job. When any link breaks, the student has nowhere to take the grievance except the street.

Students protest about many things, and some of it is politics in the ordinary sense: campus elections, national controversies, ideological contests between student wings of political parties. That protest is part of democratic life and is not the subject of this essay. The subject is the larger and steadier current: young people who have spent years preparing for an examination run by a public body, and who find that the examination was leaked, cancelled, postponed, or never completed. For that student, the protest is not a statement. It is the only way to file a complaint the state has given no other address for.

Six findings anchor this analysis:

  1. The largest student mobilisations are about the state as examiner and employer. The Indian Express investigation of February 2024 documented 41 leaks in recruitment examinations over five years across 15 states, affecting about 1.4 crore applicants competing for just over 1.04 lakh posts. That is roughly 135 applicants for every post whose process was disrupted. The leaks were not confined to one party’s governments.
  2. The wait after a failure is measured in years, not weeks. In at least 15 of the 41 cases the Indian Express examined, candidates waited nearly a year for a re-examination. In four the wait crossed two years. In seven, candidates were still waiting at the time of reporting. For an applicant near an age limit, a cancelled examination can be a lost attempt that is never returned.
  3. No one counts the failures officially, and the private counts disagree. India Today reported 70 leaks in seven years affecting about 1.7 crore applicants across 15 states. Newslaundry’s ten-year count identified 89 suspected cases and 48 retests affecting at least 6.5 crore candidates. The Tribune reported more than 50 cases since 2015, including 14 in Rajasthan and 14 in Gujarat. The figures differ because the periods, definitions and sources differ. That is the point: the most consequential failures of the public examination system are tallied by newsrooms, not by the state.
  4. The law that followed punishes cheating; it does not publish the record. The Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 5 February 2024 and became law that year. It creates offences and penalties for examinations conducted by central bodies. It does not require any authority to publish, examination by examination, what was notified, held, cancelled and re-held, or how long candidates waited. Most of the documented leaks occurred in state examinations, outside its reach.
  5. The campus grievance office was mandated and then not staffed. The UGC (Redressal of Grievances of Students) Regulations, 2023 were notified on 11 April 2023 and required every university to appoint an ombudsperson within 30 days. In January 2024 the UGC published a list of 421 universities that had not: one central, 256 state, 162 private and two deemed-to-be universities. The list fell to 228 in March 2024, 157 by 1 June 2024, and 63 in July 2024. The naming worked. It also showed that a statutory duty was ignored for most of a year until publication made the delay visible.
  6. The grievance channel is built for one student at a time. The ombudsperson hears individual complaints on admissions, fees, reservation, scholarships, ragging, examinations and results. That is the right list of subjects. But a leaked paper or a cancelled recruitment cycle is a collective grievance affecting lakhs of people at once, and no institution exists to receive it, answer it, or publish what was done.

Who protests, and about what

The student who protests over an examination is usually not a campus activist. They are an aspirant: a graduate, often in their twenties, preparing for a recruitment examination for a teaching post, a police constable’s post, a clerk’s post or a state civil service. The scale of that population is the scale of the problem. The State of Working India 2026 report from Azim Premji University puts India’s graduates aged 20 to 29 at 6.3 crore, up from 50 lakh in 1983. For a large share of them, a government post remains the most secure work available, and the examination is the only door to it.

This is why the protest so often takes the same form. It starts after a cancellation or a disputed result, moves from social media to a district headquarters or a state capital, and asks for something specific: a retest date, a published answer key, an inquiry, a relaxation of the age limit. These are administrative demands. They become political because no administrative channel exists to receive them.

Where it happens

The map of examination protest follows the map of examination failure. The Tribune’s February 2024 count named Rajasthan, Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Telangana and Uttarakhand among the states with repeated leaks in public recruitment. These are states where state recruitment boards and public service commissions run some of the largest examinations in the world by applicant count, often through private vendors for printing, logistics and online testing. The Indian Express documented papers circulated on messaging apps within minutes, papers stolen from government offices, and vendor servers allegedly compromised. Every one of those is a weak link in a chain that the state owns but does not fully control.

How the system answers now

The present answer has three parts: cancel, investigate, re-test. Each is reasonable on its own. Together they shift the whole cost of the failure onto the candidate, who pays again in time, coaching fees, travel and age. The state’s accountability ends with an arrest. The candidate’s loss continues until a new result is published, which, in more than a third of the cases the Indian Express examined, took close to a year or longer.

An earlier essay in this Review, Who Owns an Examination, examined the national entrance-test agency after the 2024 NEET controversy and argued for automatic redress. This essay is about the far larger number of state recruitment examinations, where the same failure has no national owner at all.

What the system does not do is publish. There is no public register where a candidate, a journalist or a legislator can see every recruitment examination in a state, its notification date, the date it was held, whether it was cancelled, why, and when the re-test took place. The information exists in every commission’s files. It is simply not assembled into the one ledger that would show which bodies deliver and which do not.

What the future holds

India’s neighbourhood has shown how quickly an examination-and-jobs grievance can become national politics. In Bangladesh in 2024, protests that began on campuses over quotas in government jobs grew within weeks into a movement that ended a government. In Nepal in September 2025, a youth-led protest wave forced a change of government within days. India’s institutions are deeper and its federal structure spreads pressure across many capitals, so the comparison should not be pushed too far. But the underlying arithmetic is shared: a large cohort of educated young people, a public job queue that is the main route to security, and rules that many of them no longer believe are applied fairly.

The direction of the numbers matters. The State of Working India 2026 reports that graduates made up 67 per cent of unemployed Indians aged 20 to 29 in 2023, up from 46 per cent in 2017. The companion essay examines why so much of that unemployment stays out of sight. The examination hall is where it becomes visible.

The counter-case

There is a fair argument that protest is the system working, not failing. Leaks are crimes. They are investigated, arrests follow, the 2024 Act raised penalties, and several states have legislated their own. The UGC’s own figures show compliance with the ombudsperson rule rising quickly once defaulters were named. Not every student protest is an examination grievance, and no grievance office would absorb protest that is genuinely political. On this view the state is responding, and a register adds paperwork without stopping a single leak.

The argument is half right. Enforcement matters, and naming worked for the ombudspersons. But enforcement addresses the person who leaked the paper, not the lakhs who sat it. A register would not stop a leak. It would make the cost of each one visible, attach it to a named body, and let candidates plan around a calendar the state is required to keep. That is the difference between a state that reacts to protest and one that removes the reason for it.

What we would do

  1. Publish a public examinations register in every state and at the Union. For every recruitment examination, record the notification date, the examination date, the result date, any cancellation with its stated reason, and the re-test date. Publish it as open data, updated monthly. The information already sits in every commission’s files.
  2. Bind each recruitment cycle to a published calendar with a results deadline. A commission that misses its own deadline should have to report the reason to the legislature. A calendar the state must keep is the cheapest protection a candidate can be given.
  3. Return what a cancellation takes. When an examination is cancelled for a failure on the state’s side, the affected cycle should not count against the candidate’s permitted attempts or age limit, and re-test fees should be waived automatically, not on petition.
  4. Give collective grievances an address. Each recruitment body should have a standing grievance officer with a duty to respond publicly, within a fixed period, to any representation affecting a whole examination: answer keys, cancellations, normalisation of scores, result delays.
  5. Publish what the ombudspersons hear. Every university ombudsperson under the 2023 UGC regulations should file an annual return of complaints received, resolved and pending, and the median time to resolve them. Compliance with the appointment rule rose once it was published; the same discipline should apply to performance.
  6. Count protest by cause. State police already record public order events. A short annual statement of student agitations by cause, whether examination, fees, hostel or political, would tell governments where the grievance channel is failing before the street does.

The student outside the commission office is not asking the state for a new promise. They are asking it to finish the one it already made when it notified the examination. Politics is the promise. Governance is the delivery. And for this generation, delivery starts with a result that is published on the date the state set itself.
